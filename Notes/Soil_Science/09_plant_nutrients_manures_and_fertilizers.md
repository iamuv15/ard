## SECTION 1: Historical Milestones, Classical Laws & Criteria of Essentiality

Plant nutrition developed through the identification of chemical elements necessary for plant growth and reproduction. Soil science principles govern how these elements move from the soil solution into plant tissue.

### Key Historical Discoveries & Laws

*   **Law of Minimum (1840):** Formulated by **Carl Philipp Sprengel** and popularised by **Justus von Liebig** **[KCET 2016]**. It states that *plant growth is limited not by the total resources available, but by the scarcest resource (limiting nutrient)*.
*   **Nutrient Bin Concept:** Proposed by **Whitney** to illustrate how nutrient capacity and yield limitations behave like water held in a wooden barrel with staves of unequal length.
*   **Critical Level Concept:** Developed by **Cate and Nelson** (1965) to define the soil test value or tissue nutrient concentration below which crop response to applied fertilizer is expected.
*   **Discovery of Essential Micronutrients:** **Iron (Fe)** was the first essential micronutrient discovered (E. Gris, 1843). The most recently recognized essential element is **Nickel (Ni)**, established by Eskew et al. (1983) and Brown et al. (1987).

```
          LIEBIG'S LAW OF THE MINIMUM
   [Yield capacity is constrained by the lowest stave]
   ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  N  │  P  │  K  │  Zn │  Ca │  Mg │  S  │ ... │
   │     │     │     │◄─── Limiting Nutrient (e.g., Zn)
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Criteria of Essentiality (Arnon & Stout, 1939)

**Daniel I. Arnon** and **Philip R. Stout** established three strict criteria to prove the essentiality of a chemical element for plant growth. Arnon refined these criteria in 1954:

1.  **Direct Life Cycle Completion:** A plant cannot complete its vegetative or reproductive life cycle (forming viable seeds) in the total absence of the element.
2.  **Non-Substitutability / Specificity:** The requirement for the element is completely specific; no other element can substitute for its physiological role.
3.  **Direct Metabolic Function:** The element must be directly involved in plant nutrition and metabolic processes (e.g., as a structural component, enzyme constituent, or osmotic regulator), rather than acting indirectly by neutralizing a toxic soil condition or antagonizing another element.

> 💡 **Exam Pointer:**
> * **17 Essential Nutrients:** **[IBPS-AFO 2015]** C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni.
> * **First Micronutrient Discovered:** Iron ($\text{Fe}$).
> * **Latest Essential Element:** Nickel ($\text{Ni}$) (1987).
> * **First Commercial Fertilizer Used:** Calcium Nitrate $\text{Ca(NO}_3)_2$.

---

### Functional or Metabolic Nutrients (D.J. Nicholas, 1961)

**D.J. Nicholas** introduced the term **Functional Nutrients** (or **Metabolic Nutrients**) for elements that play a role in plant metabolism, regardless of whether their role satisfies the strict Arnon and Stout criteria. 

$$\text{Functional Nutrients (21)} = \text{17 Essential Nutrients} + \text{4 Beneficial Nutrients (Na, Si, V, Co)}$$

*   **Sodium ($\text{Na}^+$):** Essential for $C_4$ plants (e.g., Amaranthus, Atriplex) and CAM plants; regulates osmotic pressure in halophytes and sugar crops (e.g., Sugar beet).
*   **Silicon ($\text{Si}$):** Imparts structural rigidity to cell walls in Poaceae (Rice, Sugarcane); reduces lodging and pest/disease incidence.
*   **Vanadium ($\text{V}$):** Functions as a substitute or complement to Molybdenum in nitrogen fixation by green algae (*Chlorella*) and free-living bacteria (*Azotobacter*).
*   **Cobalt ($\text{Co}$):** Structural component of Vitamin $B_{12}$ (Cobalamin); essential for nitrogen fixation in legume root nodules via leghaemoglobin synthesis.

### Classification Schemes

#### By Requirement Quantity
*   **Macro-nutrients ($\ge 1000\text{ mg/kg}$ or $\ge 0.1\%$ dry weight):** 
    *   *Structural:* $\text{C, H, O}$
    *   *Primary Macronutrients:* $\text{N, P, K}$
    *   *Secondary Macronutrients:* $\text{Ca, Mg, S}$
*   **Micro-nutrients ($< 100\text{ mg/kg}$ or $< 0.01\%$ dry weight):** $\text{Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni}$.

#### By Biochemical / Physiological Function (Mengel & Kirkby)
*   **Group 1 ($\text{C, H, O, N, S}$):** Constituents of organic matter, proteins, amino acids, enzymes, and nucleic acids.
*   **Group 2 ($\text{P, B}$):** Energy transfer (ATP/ADP), esterification with alcohol groups, sugar translocation.
*   **Group 3 ($\text{K, Ca, Mg, Cl}$):** Osmotic regulators, ion-balancing agents, enzyme activators, cellular signaling **[RRB SO-2018]**.
*   **Group 4 ($\text{Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, Ni}$):** Catalysts in redox systems, electron transport chains, heavy-metal protein complexes.

> ⚠️ **Common Trap:**
> * **Primary Orthophosphate ($\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$)** dominates in **acidic soils (pH < 7.0)**.
> * **Secondary Orthophosphate ($\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$)** dominates in **alkaline soils (pH > 7.0)**.
> * At **pH 7.2**, both forms exist in equal proportions ($1:1$). At extreme pH (> 9.0), Tertiary Orthophosphate ($\text{PO}_4^{3-}$) appears.

## SECTION 3: Mobility in Soil & Plant Matrix and Deficiency Symptom Mapping

Deficiency symptoms develop based on a nutrient's **phloem mobility** within the plant. Mobile nutrients are translocated from mature, older tissues to young, actively growing sink tissues. Immobile nutrients cannot be remobilized, so symptoms appear first on newly formed apical organs.

### Soil Mobility Spectrum

*   **Mobile in Soil:** $\text{NO}_3^-$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{BO}_3^{3-}$, $\text{Cl}^-$. High potential for leaching loss in wet environments.
*   **Less Mobile / Moderately Mobile in Soil:** $\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{K}^+$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$. Held by Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) complexes.
*   **Immobile in Soil:** $\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, $\text{Fe}^{2+}$. Strongly bound by soil mineral surfaces and precipitation reactions.

---

### Plant Mobility Spectrum & Deficiency Symptom Location

```
                     PLANT ARCHITECTURE & SYMPTOM LOCATION
  
           Terminal Buds / Apical Meristems:  [Ca, B]  (Immobile)
                       │
             Young / Emerging Leaves:        [Fe, Mn, Cu, S]  (Moderately Immobile)
                       │
             New/Middle Leaves (Interveinal): [Zn]  (Intermediate)
                       │
             Old / Lower Leaves:             [N, P, K, Mg, Mo]  (Highly Mobile)
```

| Plant Mobility Category | Nutrient Elements | Deficiency Symptom Location | Anatomical/Physiological Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Highly Mobile** | **$\text{N, P, K, Mg}$** | **Older / Lower Leaves** | Nutrients hydrolyze from older leaves and translocate through phloem to young developing tissues. |
| **Intermediate / Moderately Mobile** | **$\text{Zn}$** | **Middle Leaves / Older & Younger Boundary** | Limited translocation capacity; symptoms appear across middle leaves before advancing. |
| **Moderately Immobile** | **$\text{Fe, Mn, Cu, S}$** | **Young / New Leaves** | Low phloem transport; elements are bound within structural proteins or photosynthetic organelles in older tissues. |
| **Completely Immobile** | **$\text{Ca, B}$** | **Terminal Buds / Apical Growing Tips** | Immobile in phloem; dependent on xylem transpirational flow. Apical meristems die when supply is interrupted. |

## SECTION 4: Biochemical Functions, Specific Deficiency Symptoms & Toxicity Dynamics

### Macronutrients

#### 1. Nitrogen ($\text{N}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Component of amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, alkaloids, and the **porphyrin ring of chlorophyll**.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptom appears on **older/lower leaves first** **[IBPS-AFO 2016]**, **[IBPS-AFO 2017]**.
    *   Uniform chlorosis (pale green to light yellow). Leaves develop a characteristic **"V-shaped" yellowing** starting at the tip and progressing along the midrib.
    *   Stunted plant growth, thin rigid stems, premature senescence.
    *   Starvation leads to reddish-purple anthocyanin pigments in older leaves and stems.
*   **Toxicity:** Dark blue-green foliage, excessive vegetative growth, delayed maturity, weak stems prone to lodging, increased susceptibility to insect pests and fungal diseases.

#### 2. Phosphorus ($\text{P}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Structural component of nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), phospholipids, and energy transfer compounds (**ATP, ADP, AMP**). Drives root development, seed formation, early crop establishment, and energy metabolism **[IBPS-AFO 2017]**.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **older/lower leaves**.
    *   Foliage turns abnormally **dark green**, developing intense **purple or reddish coloration** due to anthocyanin accumulation.
    *   Slender stems, poor root system branching, delayed crop maturity, reduced flowering and seed setting.
*   **Crop Requirement Hierarchy:** Potato > Wheat > Maize > Rice, Millets > Legumes > Cotton, Jute **[IBPS-AFO 2018]**.

#### 3. Potassium ($\text{K}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Acts as an osmotic regulator, controls stomatal opening and closing, maintains cell turgor, and acts as an enzyme activator for over 60 photosynthetic and respiratory enzymes **[RRB SO-2018]**.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **older lower leaves**.
    *   Marginal chlorosis quickly leading to **leaf scorching, firing, and necrosis** along leaf margins.
    *   Weakened stems ("lodging" susceptibility), reduced disease resistance, smaller fruit size, diminished winter hardiness.

#### 4. Calcium ($\text{Ca}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Structural constituent of the cell wall as **Calcium Pectate** in the middle lamella. Regulates cell division, elongation, membrane integrity, and acts as a secondary messenger ($Ca^{2+}$/Calmodulin) **[RRB SO-2018]**.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear at **terminal buds and young apical tissues**.
    *   Growing points die back (**"dieback"**), terminal leaves deform with hooked tips and inverted cup margins.
    *   Gelatinous root tips, root tip necrosis.
    *   Causes **Blossom End Rot** in Tomato and Sweet Pepper, **Cavity Spot** in Carrot, and **Bitter Pit** in Apple.

#### 5. Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** **Central metallic atom in the chlorophyll porphyrin ring** **[IBPS-AFO 2017]**, **[IBPS-AFO 2018]**. Activates RuBisCO and PEPCase in carbon fixation, and stabilizes ribosome structure.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **older/lower leaves**.
    *   **Interveinal chlorosis** where leaf veins remain green while interveinal areas turn pale yellow or white.
    *   Leaves may develop brilliant red, purple, or bronze tints along margins (e.g., "Reddening" in Cotton).
    *   Leaves become brittle and cup upward along the margins.

#### 6. Sulfur ($\text{S}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Constituent of sulfur-containing amino acids (**Methionine, Cysteine, Cystine**), proteins, Ferredoxin, Coenzyme A, Biotin, and Thiamine. Important for mustard oil glucosides (Glucosinolates) in Brassicas.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **young/newer leaves first** (resembles Nitrogen deficiency, but occurs on young leaves because Sulfur is moderately immobile).
    *   Uniform pale yellow chlorosis across young foliage without early leaf drop.
    *   Stems become thin, elongated, rigid, and brittle. Delayed crop maturity.

### Micronutrients

#### 7. Iron ($\text{Fe}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Constituent of cytochromes, catalase, peroxidase, and ferredoxin. Essential for chlorophyll biosynthesis, electron transport in photosynthesis, and respiration.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Tissue concentration drops below **< 50 ppm** **[IBPS-AFO 2019]**, **[IBPS-AFO 2020]**.
    *   Symptoms appear on **young leaves**.
    *   Distinct **interveinal chlorosis**: interveinal tissue turns pale yellow/white while the entire network of veins remains dark green.
    *   In severe cases, young leaves turn ivory white and develop necrosis. Common on high pH, calcareous soils.

#### 8. Manganese ($\text{Mn}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Essential for the **photolysis of water (Hill Reaction)** in Photosystem II ($\text{PS II}$), activating carboxylases, dehydrogenases, and oxidases.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Tissue concentration drops below **< 25 ppm**.
    *   Symptoms appear on **young and middle leaves**.
    *   Interveinal chlorosis, but unlike Iron, small necrotic spots or specks appear within the yellowed interveinal bands. Veins remain green, creating a checkboard or mesh pattern.
    *   Causes **Marsh Spot** in Pea, **Pahala Blight** in Sugarcane, and **Grey Speck** in Oats.

#### 9. Zinc ($\text{Zn}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Biosynthesis of **Indole-3-Acetic Acid (IAA)** via Tryptophan synthesis. Constituent of carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase, and superoxide dismutase enzymes.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   *Most widespread micronutrient deficiency in Indian soils.*
    *   Interveinal chlorosis on **middle/young leaves** with broad bands of bleached tissue on either side of the midrib.
    *   Drastic reduction in internode length causing **rosetting** or shortened stem nodes.
    *   Reduced leaf expansion, producing narrow, clustered leaves (**"Little Leaf"** in Mango, Cashew, and Brinjal **[IBPS-AFO 2016]**; **"Mottle Leaf"** in Citrus **[IBPS-AFO 2016]**; **"Khaira Disease"** in Paddy **[IBPS-AFO 2015]**; **"White Bud"** in Maize).

#### 10. Copper ($\text{Cu}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Component of plastocyanin (electron transfer in $\text{PS I}$), cytochrome oxidase, polyphenol oxidase, and ascorbic acid oxidase. Required for lignin synthesis in cell walls. Forms strong chelate complexes with soil organic matter.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **young leaf tips and terminal shoots**.
    *   Leaf tips turn dark green, roll, twist, wither, and turn necrotic (**"Tip Rot"** or **"Reclamation Disease"** in cereals).
    *   Terminal dieback of twigs in citrus (**"Citrus Dieback"**), gum pockets under bark, and excessive tillering in cereals with empty spikes.

#### 11. Boron ($\text{B}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Translocation of sugars via sugar-borate complexes, cell wall formation (cross-linking rhamnogalacturonan-II), pollen germination, pollen tube elongation, and cell division in meristems.
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Soil concentration drops below **< 0.33 ppm**.
    *   Death of **terminal growth points / apical buds**.
    *   Leaves become thick, brittle, distorted, and curled.
    *   Breakdown of internal vascular tissue causing hollow, corky, or brown centers in storage organs (**"Heart Rot"** in Sugarbeet, **"Hollow Stem"** in Cauliflower, **"Internal Cork"** in Apple, **"Fruit Cracking"** in Pomegranate/Tomato/Apple) **[IBPS-AFO 2016]**, **[IBPS-AFO 2017]**.
    *   Primary Mineral Source: **Tourmaline** and **Borax** **[RRB SO 2019]**.

#### 12. Molybdenum ($\text{Mo}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Structural metal component of **Nitrate Reductase** (converts $\text{NO}_3^-$ to $\text{NO}_2^-$) and **Nitrogenase** (biological nitrogen fixation in Rhizobium/Azotobacter).
*   **Deficiency Symptoms:**
    *   Symptoms appear on **older/middle leaves**.
    *   Interveinal chlorosis accompanied by marginal necrosis and leaf cupping.
    *   Severe blade reduction resulting in thread-like leaf growth (**"Whiptail"** in Cauliflower and Brassicas).
    *   Inability to fix atmospheric nitrogen, producing Nitrogen starvation symptoms in legumes.

#### 13. Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$)
*   **Biochemical Role:** Acts as an osmotic solute and counter-ion. Involved in **photolytic water-splitting (Hill Reaction)** in Photosystem II alongside Manganese **[IBPS-AFO 2018]**, **[RRB SO-2018]**.

### Composting Methods & Processes

Composting is the aerobic or anaerobic biological decomposition of organic residues by thermophilic and mesophilic microorganisms.

```
                  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                  │                COMPOSTING METHODS                 │
                  └─────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┘
                                            │
               ┌────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┐
               ▼                                                         ▼
┌──────────────────────────────┐                          ┌──────────────────────────────┐
│       AEROBIC METHODS        │                          │      ANAEROBIC METHODS       │
│  (Fast, Higher N Losses)     │                          │  (Slow, High N Retention)    │
└──────────────┬───────────────┘                          └──────────────┬───────────────┘
               │                                                         │
       ┌───────┴───────┐                                                 ▼
       ▼               ▼                                         BANGALORE METHOD
 INDORE METHOD    HEAP METHOD                                   (Dr. L.N. Acharya)
(Howard & Wad)  (Turned Piles)                                 [Agri MPSC 2018]
```

#### 1. Bangalore Method of Composting
* **Pioneer / Author**: Developed by **Dr. L.N. Acharya** **[Agri MPSC 2018]**.
* **Nature**: Primarily **anaerobic decomposition** carried out in pits.
* **Process**: Pit size is typically $6\text{ m} \times 1.5\text{ m} \times 1\text{ m}$. Alternate layers of refuse and night soil/cattle dung are placed until the heap rises $15–20\text{ cm}$ above ground level. The pit is sealed with a mud plaster dome ($15\text{ cm}$ thick layer).
* **Key Advantages**:
  * No turning is required (labour-saving).
  * High nitrogen and organic matter retention due to reduced volatilization under sealed anaerobic conditions.
  * Takes **5 to 6 months** for complete decomposition; widely used for urban municipal solid waste handling.

#### 2. Indore Method of Composting
* **Pioneers**: Developed by **Sir Albert Howard** and **Y.D. Wad** at the Institute of Plant Industry, Indore.
* **Nature**: Primarily **aerobic decomposition**.
* **Process**: Organic materials are piled in trenches or heaps ($3\text{ m} \times 1.5\text{ m} \times 1\text{ m}$). Periodic turnings are strictly mandatory to maintain oxygen levels:
  * **1st Turning**: $10–15\text{ days}$ after filling.
  * **2nd Turning**: $30\text{ days}$ after filling.
  * **3rd Turning**: $60\text{ days}$ after filling.
* **Key Advantages**: Rapid decomposition (**3 to 4 months**); high operational heat ($60–70^\circ\text{C}$) destroys weed seeds and human pathogens. High loss of $N$ via volatilization occurs during physical turnings.

#### 3. Heap / Coimbatore Method
* **Nature**: Aerobic followed by anaerobic decomposition in raised heaps above ground level (suitable for high rainfall/shallow water table areas).
* **Process**: A base layer of farm waste is covered with dung slurry, repeated up to a height of $1.5\text{ m}$, then plastered with mud. Left undisturbed for **4 to 5 months**.

> 💡 **Exam Pointer:**
> * Growth of ***Azotobacter*** or ***Aspergillus niger*** on soil cultures reflects specific **nutrient deficiencies** (particularly $N$ and $P$) in the tested substrate.
> * Soil Microbes possess the **lowest C:N ratio** ($4:1$ to $9:1$) among all organic sources **[RRB SO 2020]**.
> * Soil Organic Carbon ($\text{OC}$) serves as a direct indicator of available Nitrogen in soil **[AP VAA-2019]**, **[IFFCO AGT 2019]**.

> ⚠️ **Common Trap:**
> Do not confuse the composting pioneer of the Bangalore Method (**Dr. L.N. Acharya** **[Agri MPSC 2018]**) with the pioneer of the USLE equation (Wischmeier and Smith) or the Law of Minimum (J.V. Liebig **[KCET 2016]**).

## 2. Chemical Fertilizers – Classification, Composition & Properties

A fertilizer is a manufactured chemical product containing essential plant nutrients in inorganic or synthetic organic forms.


#### Hygroscopicity Sequence
Hygroscopicity is the relative capacity of a fertilizer material to absorb moisture from the surrounding atmosphere at a specific relative humidity ($RH$).

```
                      HIGHEST HYGROSCOPICITY
                                │
                    Ammonium Nitrate [NH4NO3]
                                │
                            Urea [CO(NH2)2]
                                │
                 Ammonium Sulfate [(NH4)2SO4]
                                │
            Calcium Ammonium Nitrate (CAN) [CaCO3·NH4NO3]
                                │
                      LOWEST HYGROSCOPICITY
```

$$\text{Ammonium Nitrate} > \text{Urea} > \text{Ammonium Sulfate} > \text{Calcium Ammonium Nitrate (CAN)}$$

> 💡 **Exam Pointer:**
> * **Sodium Nitrate ($\text{NaNO}_3$)** destroys soil granulation and aggregate stability due to the dispersing action of $\text{Na}^+$ ions.
> * **Calcium Ammonium Nitrate (CAN)** aids in the development and maintenance of good soil structural aggregates.

## 3. Equivalent Acidity and Basicity of Fertilizers

Continuous application of chemical fertilizers leaves an acidic or basic residue in the soil.

* **Equivalent Acidity**: The quantity of Calcium Carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) in parts by weight required to neutralize the acid residue resulting from the application of $100\text{ kg}$ of an acidic fertilizer.
* **Equivalent Basicity**: The residual basicity produced by $100\text{ kg}$ of a basic fertilizer, expressed as the equivalent weight of Calcium Carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) in kilograms.

```
                      ACIDIC RESIDUAL NATURE (Equivalent Acidity Value)
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Anhydrous NH3 (148) > NH4Cl (128) > (NH4)2SO4 (110) > Urea (80-84) > NH4NO3 (60) > DAP (77) │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

                      BASIC RESIDUAL NATURE (Equivalent Basicity Value)
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 Calcium Cyanamide (63) > NaNO3 (29) > DCP (25) > Ca(NO3)2 (21)              │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```
